Getting Out the Door Without a Fight: Why Leaving the House Is So Hard

For children ages 2–8 · 5 min read

It doesn't matter where you're going. The zoo, the dentist, a birthday party your child has been excited about for a week — the ten minutes before you actually get out the door can be some of the hardest of the day, regardless of how appealing the destination is.

That's worth sitting with for a second, because it tells you something useful: if a child resists leaving for a fun outing just as much as a dreaded one, the destination isn't really the problem. The leaving itself is the problem — and that's a much more specific, more solvable thing.

What's actually hard about leaving

Leaving the house asks a child to stop something — whatever they were doing, which from their perspective was probably going fine — and start a sequence of physical tasks that are themselves genuinely difficult: finding shoes, getting them on the right feet, wrestling into a coat, gathering whatever they're bringing. None of that is optional or quick for a lot of young children, and all of it happens while a parent is standing there, increasingly impatient, at the exact moment patience is running thin on both sides.

There's also no natural stopping point tied to leaving. Unlike a meal ending or a show finishing, "it's time to go" can land at literally any moment in the middle of something — which makes it feel arbitrary even when, to the adult, the timing makes complete sense.

Five things that genuinely help

1. Give an early warning tied to something concrete

"We're leaving in five minutes" means little to a young child mid-play. "Two more pieces of the puzzle, then we put on shoes" gives them something they can actually track and finish. The warning needs to land while they're still absorbed, not after you've already said "let's go" the first time.

2. Use the same getting-ready sequence every time

Shoes, then coat, then bag, then out — the same order, regardless of where you're headed. A consistent sequence becomes something your child's body learns to move through with less resistance over time, the same way a bedtime routine becomes automatic after enough repetitions.

3. Let them carry something that's theirs

A small bag, a favourite toy, a chosen snack — something your child is in charge of carrying gives them a role in the process of leaving, rather than just being moved through it. It's a small shift from passive to active that tends to make a real difference.

4. Practise shoes and coats outside the rush

If putting on shoes is consistently the sticking point, a few minutes of low-stakes practice on a weekend afternoon — no deadline, no destination, just the physical task — builds the skill and the tolerance separately from the pressure of actually needing to leave. A task practised calmly is easier to do quickly later.

5. Once you start moving, keep moving

Once the sequence has begun, try not to pause to negotiate or re-explain. Narrate simply and steadily — "shoes now, then we go" — and keep the momentum going. Stopping to argue mid-sequence often resets the whole process and makes the next attempt harder, not easier.

When it's urgent and there's no time to prep

Some departures genuinely can't be planned for — an unexpected need to leave right now doesn't leave room for warnings or practice. On those days, keep your own voice calm and your instructions simple and physical ("shoes, I'll help") rather than trying to explain the reason for the rush. Save the conversation about what happened for later, once you're both out the door and the pressure is off.

Common questions

My child needs twenty minutes just to get shoes on — is that normal?

It's common, especially for younger children or children who find fine motor tasks or transitions genuinely hard. If it happens consistently, practising the specific task outside of a time crunch often shortens it more effectively than trying to hurry through it in the moment. If the struggle seems out of proportion to your child's age, it's reasonable to mention it at a routine check-up.

Does this approach work for toddlers who can't manage buttons or zippers yet?

Yes — the sequence and the role can scale down. A toddler's "job" might just be holding their own shoes or picking which jacket, while you handle the fine motor parts. The goal at any age is the same: a predictable order and some genuine participation, sized to what your child can actually do.

What if my child resists leaving even for things they say they want to do?

This is genuinely common and it's a good sign the resistance isn't about the destination. Most children who fight leaving for a fun outing settle quickly once they're actually there. The struggle is almost always about stopping the current activity, not about the plan ahead — which is exactly why a consistent leaving routine helps regardless of where you're headed.
